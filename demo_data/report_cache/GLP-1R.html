1. Target Profile & Biological Function

GLP-1R (glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor) is a G protein-coupled receptor that plays a central role in glucose homeostasis and insulin secretion. Glp1r-null mice are viable and fertile with normal body weight on standard chow but exhibit mild glucose intolerance and a blunted insulin secretory response to oral glucose, consistent with GLP-1R's incretin role [R-9]. The knockout mice also show altered gastric emptying kinetics in some studies, and no embryonic lethality or major structural malformation has been reported [R-9]. The viable, mild knockout phenotype indicates that GLP-1R loss-of-function is not catastrophic and supports interpreting on-target metabolic effects such as gastrointestinal and glycemic changes as manageable or adaptive rather than representing fundamental developmental liability [R-9].

Multiple approved drugs act as GLP-1R agonists for the treatment of type 2 diabetes mellitus and obesity, including liraglutide [R-1], semaglutide [R-5, R-6], dulaglutide [R-3], exenatide [R-8], tirzepatide [R-2], and lixisenatide (in combination with insulin glargine) [R-7]. These agents are administered by subcutaneous injection or, in the case of oral semaglutide, by oral tablet formulation [R-5]. The class functions by activating GLP-1R to enhance glucose-dependent insulin secretion, suppress glucagon secretion, and slow gastric emptying.

A consistent finding across the GLP-1R agonist class is the induction of thyroid C-cell tumors in rodent carcinogenicity studies. Liraglutide causes dose-dependent and treatment-duration-dependent thyroid C-cell tumors at clinically relevant exposures in both genders of rats and mice [R-1, PRIMARY FINDING]. Similarly, semaglutide induced statistically significant increases in thyroid C-cell adenomas and carcinomas in both mice and rats at all dose levels tested, at clinically relevant exposures [R-5, R-6]. Dulaglutide caused dose-related and treatment-duration-dependent increases in thyroid C-cell adenomas and carcinomas in rats at ≥3-fold the maximum recommended human dose based on AUC [R-3]. Tirzepatide produced statistically significant increases in thyroid C-cell adenomas in male and female rats at all doses examined (0.1- to 1-fold the maximum recommended human dose) [R-2], and exenatide induced benign thyroid C-cell adenomas in female rats at systemic exposures 5- to 130-times the human exposure [R-8]. The human relevance of these rodent thyroid C-cell tumors remains unknown and could not be determined by clinical or nonclinical studies [R-3, R-5, R-6, PRIMARY FINDING]. Importantly, this thyroid C-cell finding represents a gain-of-function pharmacologic effect of receptor agonism, not a loss-of-function effect, and thus the knockout precedent does not inform interpretation of this specific toxicologic finding [R-9].

3. Class Effects & Known Target Toxicities

The most prominent recurring toxicity across GLP-1 receptor agonists is the development of thyroid C-cell tumors in rodent carcinogenicity studies. Liraglutide caused dose-related increases in benign thyroid C-cell adenomas at 1 and 3 mg/kg/day in mice (incidences 13-19% in males, 6-20% in females), with treatment-related malignant C-cell carcinomas occurring in 3% of females at the highest dose [R-1]. Tirzepatide produced statistically significant increases in thyroid C-cell adenomas at all doses examined in male and female rats (≥0.1-fold the maximum recommended human dose based on AUC), with increased C-cell adenomas and carcinomas combined observed at doses as low as 0.15 mg/kg [R-2]. Dulaglutide caused dose-related and treatment-duration-dependent increases in thyroid C-cell tumors (adenomas and/or carcinomas) in rats at ≥3-fold the maximum recommended human dose, with statistically significant C-cell adenomas at ≥0.5 mg/kg and numerical increases in carcinomas at 5 mg/kg [R-3]. Semaglutide showed statistically significant increases in thyroid C-cell adenomas in both male and female mice and rats at all dose levels tested (>3-fold human exposure), with statistically significant increases in C-cell carcinomas in male rats at ≥0.01 mg/kg/day at clinically relevant exposures [R-5, R-6]. Exenatide produced benign thyroid C-cell adenomas in female rats at all doses tested (5- to 130-times human exposure), with incidences of 14%, 11%, and 23% in the low-, medium-, and high-dose groups compared to 8% and 5% in controls [R-8]. The human relevance of these rodent thyroid C-cell tumors remains unknown and could not be determined by clinical or nonclinical studies [R-3, R-5, R-6].

A secondary class-specific finding is injection-site pathology. Liraglutide produced a treatment-related increase in fibrosarcomas on the dorsal skin and subcutis at injection sites in male mice at 3 mg/kg/day, attributed to high local drug concentration near the injection site, noting that the clinical formulation concentration (6 mg/mL) is 10-times higher than the concentration used in the mouse carcinogenicity study [R-1]. Insulin glargine (formulated with lixisenatide in one product) produced histiocytomas at injection sites in male rats and male mice in acid vehicle-containing groups, though these were not observed in female animals or in saline control groups [R-7].

4. Regulatory & Clinical Precedents

Multiple GLP-1 receptor agonists have gained regulatory approval for metabolic indications, including liraglutide (approved as Victoza for type 2 diabetes, Saxenda for weight management), semaglutide (approved as Ozempic for type 2 diabetes, Rybelsus as oral formulation, and Wegovy for weight management), dulaglutide (Trulicity), tirzepatide (Mounjaro for type 2 diabetes, Zepbound for weight management), and exenatide (Byetta). Combination products incorporating GLP-1R agonists include insulin degludec/liraglutide (Xultophy 100/3.6) and insulin glargine/lixisenatide (Soliqua 100/33) [R-1, R-2, R-3, R-4, R-5, R-6, R-7, R-8].

All approved GLP-1R agonists carry a boxed warning for risk of thyroid C-cell tumors based on rodent carcinogenicity findings. Liraglutide caused dose-dependent and treatment-duration-dependent thyroid C-cell adenomas and carcinomas at clinically relevant exposures in both genders of rats and mice, with incidences of 13-19% adenomas in males and 6-20% in females at higher doses, plus 3% malignant C-cell carcinomas in high-dose females [R-1]. Semaglutide produced statistically significant increases in thyroid C-cell adenomas in males and females at all dose levels (≥3 times human exposure), plus significant C-cell carcinoma increases in male rats at ≥0.01 mg/kg/day at clinically relevant exposures [R-5, R-6]. Dulaglutide caused dose-related increases in C-cell tumors at ≥3-fold the maximum recommended human dose, with statistically significant adenomas at ≥0.5 mg/kg and numerical carcinoma increases at 24 times the MRHD [R-3]. Tirzepatide showed statistically significant C-cell adenomas in males (≥0.5 mg/kg) and females (≥0.15 mg/kg), with combined adenomas/carcinomas increased at all doses examined [R-2]. Exenatide produced benign thyroid C-cell adenomas in female rats at all doses, with incidences of 14%, 11%, and 23% versus 5-8% in controls at 5-, 22-, and 130-times human exposure [R-8].

The boxed warning explicitly states that human relevance of liraglutide-induced rodent thyroid C-cell tumors has not been determined, and it is unknown whether liraglutide causes thyroid C-cell tumors, including medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC), in humans [R-1]. Identical language regarding unknown human relevance appears in labeling for semaglutide and dulaglutide [R-3, R-5, R-6]. All products are contraindicated in patients with personal or family history of MTC and in patients with Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia syndrome type 2 (MEN 2), with label guidance that routine serum calcitonin monitoring or thyroid ultrasound is of uncertain value for early MTC detection [R-1]. Additional nonclinical findings include fibrosarcomas at injection sites with liraglutide attributed to high local drug concentration [R-1], and in fertility studies, female rats treated with tirzepatide showed prolonged diestrus, decreased corpora lutea, and reduced implantation sites and viable embryos at all dose levels, secondary to pharmacology [R-2].

6. Nonclinical Safety Recommendations & CTD-M2.4-Bridge

The thyroid C-cell tumor finding documented across liraglutide [R-1], tirzepatide [R-2], dulaglutide [R-3], semaglutide [R-5, R-6], and exenatide [R-8] presents four competing interpretive frameworks for CTD Module 2.4, each implying distinct nonclinical recommendations. All four perspectives agree on the core facts: dose-dependent and treatment-duration-dependent C-cell adenomas occur at exposures ranging from 0.1–0.2× MRHD (tirzepatide [R-2], liraglutide [R-1]) to ≥0.8× MRHD (semaglutide [R-5, R-6]), with carcinomas observed at higher multiples or only in specific sex/species combinations. Negative genotoxicity batteries [R-3, R-5, R-6, R-8] and negative rasH2 transgenic mouse studies [R-2, R-3] support a non-genotoxic, receptor-mediated mechanism. The four interpretations diverge on human translatability and therefore on the scope of additional nonclinical work required.

Under the **adverse** interpretation, CTD-M2.4 must address whether the clinically relevant exposure margins (as low as 0.1× MRHD for tirzepatide [R-2]) preclude establishing a safe NOAEL for C-cell neoplasia, and whether the class-wide reproducibility across five chemically distinct GLP-1R agonists constitutes a target-intrinsic oncogenic liability. Open questions include: (1) whether mechanistic studies comparing GLP-1R expression levels and calcitonin secretory kinetics in rodent versus human C-cells would clarify translatability or merely confirm species difference without resolving human risk; (2) whether postmarketing surveillance data (number of patient-years, MTC incidence rates, nested case-control analyses) have been systematically pooled across liraglutide, semaglutide, dulaglutide, tirzepatide, and exenatide to assess for any signal above population baseline; (3) whether the contraindication for patients with personal or family history of MTC or MEN 2 [R-1, R-5, R-6] should be extended to broader screening criteria or whether the acknowledged uncertain value of routine calcitonin monitoring [R-1, R-5, R-6] justifies no additional clinical monitoring recommendations. This interpretation would support inclusion of C-cell histopathology and calcitonin endpoints in chronic repeat-dose toxicology studies for any future GLP-1R agonist, along with explicit discussion of the inability to rule out low-level human risk despite mechanistic rationale.

Under the **non-adverse** interpretation, CTD-M2.4 would emphasize the documented absence of functional GLP-1R expression in human C-cells as mechanistic justification for species-specificity, referencing the regulatory acknowledgment that "human relevance of thyroid C-cell tumors in rats is unknown and could not be determined by clinical studies or nonclinical studies" [R-3, R-5, R-6]. Open questions include: (1) whether published or unpublished immunohistochemistry, in situ hybridization, or functional receptor-binding data in human thyroid tissue can be cited to quantitatively demonstrate the receptor expression differential; (2) whether >15 years of cumulative clinical exposure (exact patient-years not provided in excerpts) across five approved drugs constitutes sufficient negative evidence to downweight the rodent signal in benefit-risk assessment; (3) whether alternative nonclinical models (e.g., GLP-1R-humanized mice with human C-cell receptor expression knocked in, or cynomolgus monkey chronic studies with C-cell proliferation endpoints) have been attempted or would add value given the mechanistic disconnect. This interpretation would support standard ICH carcinogenicity study designs without additional C-cell-focused mechanistic studies, framing the boxed warning as a conservative regulatory posture rather than reflection of demonstrated human hazard.

Under the **adaptive** interpretation, CTD-M2.4 must distinguish between early compensatory C-cell hyperplasia (potentially reversible) and late neoplastic transformation (irreversible). Open questions include: (1) whether time-course studies with interim necropsies at 4, 13, 26, and 52 weeks, incorporating BrdU or Ki-67 proliferation markers and calcitonin immunostaining, could define a transition point from adaptive to neoplastic proliferation; (2) whether dose-interruption or washout arms in chronic studies could demonstrate reversibility of early C-cell changes, informing whether intermittent dosing regimens would mitigate risk; (3) whether the fertile, viable knockout phenotype [R-9] supports framing on-target metabolic effects (GI, glycemic) as adaptive but provides no insight into the gain-of-function C-cell proliferation pathway, which requires separate mechanistic analysis. This interpretation would support expanded histopathology sampling (multiple thyroid sections per animal, quantitative C-cell counts, grading of hyperplasia severity) in standard 2-year studies, with statistical modeling of dose-duration-incidence relationships to define thresholds for adaptive versus adverse responses.

Under the **artifact** interpretation, CTD-M2.4 would justify why the rodent 2-year carcinogenicity study, though ICH-required, may lack predictive validity for this target, given that the test system (rodent C-cell) expresses a receptor absent in the human homolog. Open questions include: (1) whether regulatory precedent exists for discounting a positive rodent carcinogenicity signal based on mechanistic species difference, and whether such precedent is applicable here; (2) whether alternative study designs—such as primate chronic toxicity studies with thyroid monitoring—would provide more translatable safety data, and whether such studies have been conducted but are not reflected in approved labeling; (3) whether the injection-site fibrosarcomas in liraglutide mice [R-1], attributed to formulation concentration (6 mg/mL clinical vs. 0.6 mg/mL study), require reformulation risk assessment or represent a separate artifact of bolus injection trauma. This interpretation would support minimal additional nonclinical work beyond ICH core battery, framing the boxed warning as a regulatory labeling artifact driven by FDA policy on rodent C-cell findings rather than a scientifically supported human hazard signal.

No additional reproductive toxicology studies are indicated, as fertility effects in tirzepatide [R-2] were secondary to pharmacologic body-weight and estrous-cycle changes, and other compounds showed no fertility impact [R-3, R-5, R-6, R-8]. The knockout phenotype [R-9]—viable, fertile, mild glucose intolerance—supports interpreting on-target metabolic modulation as manageable but does not inform the C-cell proliferation question, which arises from pharmacologic receptor agonism rather than loss of function. CTD-M2.4 must explicitly delineate this distinction to prevent conflation of loss-of-function precedent with gain-of-function toxicity findings.
