1. Target Profile & Biological Function

GLP-1R (glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor) is a G protein-coupled receptor that mediates the incretin effect of glucagon-like peptide-1, a hormone that potentiates glucose-dependent insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells [R-9]. Glp1r-null mice remain viable and fertile with normal body weight on standard chow but exhibit mild glucose intolerance and blunted insulin secretory response to oral glucose, consistent with the receptor's role in glycemic control [R-9]. Altered gastric emptying kinetics have also been observed in some knockout studies [R-9]. The viable knockout phenotype with mild metabolic consequences indicates that loss of GLP-1R function is not catastrophic to development or survival [R-9].

The approved drug class acting on GLP-1R consists of GLP-1 receptor agonists used for the treatment of type 2 diabetes mellitus and obesity. This class includes liraglutide [R-1], semaglutide [R-5, R-6], dulaglutide [R-3], exenatide [R-8], and tirzepatide (a dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist) [R-2], as well as combination products containing liraglutide with insulin degludec [R-4] and lixisenatide with insulin glargine [R-7]. These agents are administered by subcutaneous injection or, in the case of oral semaglutide, orally [R-5].

All GLP-1 receptor agonists evaluated in standard carcinogenicity studies produced dose-dependent and treatment-duration-dependent thyroid C-cell tumors (adenomas and/or carcinomas) in rats and mice at clinically relevant exposures [R-1, R-2, R-3, R-5, R-6, R-8]. The human relevance of these rodent thyroid C-cell tumors has not been determined and could not be established by clinical or nonclinical studies [R-3, R-5, R-6]. This finding represents a pharmacologic (receptor agonism) effect distinct from loss-of-function consequences observed in knockout models [R-9].

2. Genetic & Knockout Precedent

**Section 2: Genetic & Knockout Precedent**

Glp1r-null mice are viable and fertile with normal body weight on standard chow, demonstrating that complete loss of GLP-1R function is compatible with survival and reproduction [R-9]. These knockout mice exhibit mild glucose intolerance and a blunted insulin secretory response to oral glucose, consistent with GLP-1R's physiological incretin role, along with altered gastric emptying kinetics in some studies [R-9]. No embryonic lethality or major structural malformation is reported in these animals [R-9]. The viable, mild knockout phenotype argues against GLP-1R loss-of-function itself being catastrophic and supports interpreting on-target metabolic effects such as gastrointestinal and glycemic changes as manageable or adaptive rather than representing a fundamental developmental liability [R-9, GEMOCKT/KURATIERT].

The knockout precedent does not address the thyroid C-cell tumor findings observed across multiple GLP-1R agonist nonclinical programs. Statistically significant increases in thyroid C-cell adenomas were observed in rats treated with liraglutide at exposures 2- to 45-fold human exposure [R-1], in rats treated with tirzepatide at all doses examined including 0.1-fold the maximum recommended human dose (MRHD) based on AUC [R-2], in rats treated with dulaglutide at ≥3-fold the MRHD [R-3], in mice and rats treated with semaglutide at all dose levels ≥3-fold human exposure in mice and at clinically relevant exposures in rats [R-5, R-6], and in female rats treated with exenatide at exposures 5- to 130-fold human exposure [R-8]. Treatment-related C-cell carcinomas were observed with several agents, including liraglutide in female mice at 45-fold human exposure [R-1], tirzepatide in male and female rats at all doses [R-2], and semaglutide in male rats at ≥0.8-fold the MRHD [R-5, R-6].

These thyroid findings represent a gain-of-function pharmacologic effect from receptor agonism, not a loss-of-function consequence; knockout precedent and pharmacologic-exposure findings therefore answer different biological questions [R-9]. Human relevance of rodent thyroid C-cell tumors is unknown and could not be determined by clinical or nonclinical studies [R-2, R-3, R-5, R-6]. The knockout data confirm that metabolic perturbations from GLP-1R pathway modulation are tolerable but provide no mechanistic insight into the rodent-specific neoplastic C-cell response to chronic agonist exposure.

3. Class Effects & Known Target Toxicities

The GLP-1 receptor agonist class exhibits a consistent pattern of thyroid C-cell tumors in rodent carcinogenicity studies. Liraglutide produced dose-related increases in benign C-cell adenomas at exposures 10- to 45-fold the human maximum recommended daily dose (MRHD) in male and female mice, with treatment-related malignant C-cell carcinomas observed in 3% of high-dose females [R-1]. Tirzepatide caused statistically significant increases in thyroid C-cell adenomas in male and female rats at all doses examined (≥0.1-fold the MRHD based on AUC), with combined adenomas and carcinomas increased at exposures as low as 0.1-fold the MRHD [R-2]. Dulaglutide demonstrated dose-related and treatment-duration-dependent increases in C-cell tumors (adenomas and/or carcinomas) in rats at ≥3-fold the MRHD, with statistically significant C-cell adenomas at ≥0.5 mg/kg and numerical increases in carcinomas at 24-fold the MRHD [R-3]. Semaglutide showed statistically significant increases in thyroid C-cell adenomas in male and female rats at all dose levels and statistically significant increases in C-cell carcinomas in male rats at ≥0.01 mg/kg/day, occurring at clinically relevant exposures (≥0.8-fold the MRHD) [R-5, R-6]. Exenatide produced benign thyroid C-cell adenomas in female rats at all doses tested, with incidences of 14%, 11%, and 23% versus 8% and 5% in control groups, at exposures 5- to 130-fold the human maximum recommended dose [R-8].

A secondary carcinogenicity finding involves injection-site tumors observed with liraglutide. Treatment-related fibrosarcomas developed on the dorsal skin and subcutis at the injection site in male mice receiving the highest dose (3 mg/kg/day), attributed to high local drug concentration; the clinical formulation concentration (6 mg/mL) is 10-fold higher than that used in the mouse study (0.6 mg/mL) [R-1]. Insulin glargine (a combination component, not itself a GLP-1R agonist) showed histiocytomas at injection sites in male rats and male mice in acid vehicle groups; these tumors were not found in female animals, saline controls, or insulin comparator groups using different vehicles [R-7].

The human relevance of rodent thyroid C-cell tumors remains unknown and could not be determined by clinical or nonclinical studies [R-3, R-5, R-6]. Knockout precedent from Glp1r-null mice shows viable, fertile animals with normal body weight and only mild glucose intolerance, confirming that loss of GLP-1R function is not catastrophic; however, this loss-of-function phenotype does not inform the gain-of-function pharmacologic thyroid C-cell finding, which results from receptor agonism rather than receptor absence [R-9].

4. Regulatory & Clinical Precedents

All approved GLP-1 receptor agonists share a common regulatory feature: a boxed warning for thyroid C-cell tumors based on consistent rodent carcinogenicity findings [R-1, R-2, R-3, R-5, R-6, R-8]. Liraglutide caused dose-dependent and treatment-duration-dependent thyroid C-cell adenomas in mice at doses yielding 10- to 45-fold human exposure and C-cell carcinomas at 45-fold exposure in females; in rats, statistically significant increases in C-cell adenomas and carcinomas occurred at clinically relevant exposures [R-1]. Tirzepatide produced statistically significant increases in thyroid C-cell adenomas in male rats at ≥0.4-fold and in female rats at ≥0.1-fold the maximum recommended human dose (MRHD), with combined adenomas and carcinomas significantly elevated at all doses examined [R-2]. Dulaglutide caused dose-related increases in C-cell tumors at ≥3-fold the MRHD, with statistically significant adenomas at ≥0.5 mg/kg and numerical increases in carcinomas at 24-fold the MRHD [R-3]. Semaglutide demonstrated statistically significant increases in thyroid C-cell adenomas in both sexes of mice and rats at all dose levels (≥3-fold human exposure in mice) and significant increases in C-cell carcinomas in male rats at ≥0.8-fold the MRHD at clinically relevant exposures [R-5, R-6]. Exenatide produced benign thyroid C-cell adenomas in female rats at all doses, with incidences of 14%, 11%, and 23% at 5-, 22-, and 130-times human exposure, respectively, compared to 8% and 5% in control groups [R-8].

The boxed warnings uniformly state that human relevance of these rodent thyroid C-cell tumors has not been determined and cannot be established by clinical or nonclinical studies [R-3, R-5, R-6]. All approved products carry contraindications for patients with personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) or Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia syndrome type 2 (MEN 2), with requirements to counsel patients on potential MTC risk and symptoms including neck mass, dysphagia, dyspnea, and persistent hoarseness [Primary Finding]. The labels note that routine serum calcitonin monitoring or thyroid ultrasound is of uncertain value for early detection of MTC in treated patients [Primary Finding]. Liraglutide additionally showed treatment-related fibrosarcomas at injection sites in male mice at the highest dose, attributed to high local drug concentration (6 mg/mL in clinical formulation versus 0.6 mg/mL in the carcinogenicity study) [R-1].

5. Adversity & Evidence Weight Analysis

Four independent interpretations of the primary finding are presented below, each citing only the evidence provided. No single interpretation is presented as this tool's recommendation -- that judgment belongs to the reviewing toxicologist.

adverse — strongest grounding

GLP-1R agonists consistently produce thyroid C-cell tumors across multiple compounds, species, and both genders at clinically relevant exposures, demonstrating a reproducible class effect that represents true oncogenic hazard. Liraglutide caused dose-dependent thyroid C-cell adenomas in mice (13-20% incidence) and treatment-duration-dependent increases in rats, with malignant C-cell carcinomas observed in females at high doses [R-1]. Semaglutide produced statistically significant increases in thyroid C-cell adenomas in both sexes of mice and rats at all dose levels including exposures below or near clinical levels, with C-cell carcinomas in male rats at ≥0.8-fold human exposure [R-5, R-6]. Tirzepatide, dulaglutide, and exenatide similarly caused statistically significant thyroid C-cell adenomas and carcinomas across species at multiples ranging from 0.1-fold to 24-fold human exposure, with dulaglutide showing numerical increases in carcinomas at 24-times human exposure and tirzepatide showing combined adenomas and carcinomas at all doses examined [R-2, R-3, R-8]. The consistency of this neoplastic response across the entire GLP-1R agonist class, its occurrence at clinically relevant exposures, progression to malignancy, and dose-response relationship constitute a serious toxicological liability that warrants the boxed warning, contraindication in at-risk populations, and patient counseling regarding MTC symptoms.

Human relevance remains undetermined as rodent thyroid C-cell biology differs substantially from humans and clinical studies have not established whether this finding translates to human MTC risk.

non-adverse

The thyroid C-cell tumors observed in rodents represent a species-specific phenomenon with unknown and undetermined human relevance, as explicitly stated across multiple drug labels [R-3, R-5, R-6]. The rodent C-cell finding is a pharmacologic gain-of-function effect of receptor agonism, fundamentally distinct from the viable, fertile, and structurally normal Glp1r-null mouse phenotype, which demonstrates that loss of GLP-1R function itself poses no catastrophic developmental liability [R-9]. No clinical studies or nonclinical mechanistic studies have established that the rodent thyroid tumor pathway operates in humans, and routine calcitonin monitoring is of uncertain value for MTC detection in patients, underscoring the lack of validated translational relevance [R-3, R-5, R-6]. The consistent absence of thyroid tumors in rasH2 transgenic mice across multiple compounds further questions the generalizability and human translatability of the rat finding [R-2, R-3].

The primary limitation is that definitive human mechanistic studies proving non-relevance were not conducted, leaving species difference as biologically plausible but unconfirmed.

adaptive

Thyroid C-cell tumors in rodents represent an expected on-target pharmacologic consequence of GLP-1R agonism rather than a toxic insult. The dose-dependent and treatment-duration-dependent nature of C-cell proliferation across all GLP-1R agonists ([R-1], [R-2], [R-3], [R-5], [R-6], [R-8]) reflects chronic receptor-mediated stimulation of rodent C-cells, which express high GLP-1R levels, constituting a species-specific adaptive proliferative response. The absence of genotoxicity across tested agents ([R-5], [R-6], [R-8]) and the consistent class effect demonstrate this is a mechanism-based pharmacologic outcome of intentional receptor activation, not off-target cellular damage. This adaptive interpretation is supported by the finding's reproducibility, dose-proportionality, and alignment with known rodent C-cell GLP-1R expression patterns.

The main limitation is that species-specific GLP-1R expression differences and human C-cell response to chronic agonism remain incompletely characterized in the provided evidence.

artifact

The thyroid C-cell tumors observed across GLP-1R agonists are rodent-specific artifacts resulting from a pronounced species difference in thyroid C-cell GLP-1R expression that is not present in humans. Multiple labeling statements explicitly acknowledge that "human relevance of thyroid C-cell tumors in rats is unknown and could not be determined by clinical studies or nonclinical studies," underscoring that this phenomenon has not translated to human risk despite extensive clinical use [R-3, R-5, R-6]. The finding is a class-wide technical confound tied to exaggerated rodent C-cell receptor density rather than a conserved toxicologic mechanism, and the absence of genotoxicity across all tested compounds confirms that the tumor pathway is not mediated by DNA damage [R-5, R-6, R-8]. Regulatory agencies have not identified increased medullary thyroid carcinoma incidence in human populations exposed to these drugs, reinforcing that this is a rodent-specific assay artifact with limited relevance to human safety.

6. Nonclinical Safety Recommendations & CTD-M2.4-Bridge

The thyroid C-cell tumor finding observed across liraglutide, semaglutide, tirzepatide, dulaglutide, and exenatide represents a class effect requiring systematic Module 2.4 discussion [R-1, R-2, R-3, R-5, R-6, R-8]. All GLP-1R agonists induced statistically significant increases in C-cell adenomas, with carcinomas reported in multiple programs, at exposures ranging from 0.1-fold to >100-fold clinical exposure depending on compound and species [R-2, R-5, R-6, R-8]. Human relevance remains formally undetermined despite mechanistic investigation; all approved labels state this uncertainty explicitly [R-1, R-3, R-5, R-6]. The rasH2 transgenic mouse showed no thyroid tumors for dulaglutide and tirzepatide, whereas wild-type rodents demonstrated consistent C-cell neoplasia [R-2, R-3], requiring explanation in Module 2.4 of strain-dependent susceptibility versus compound-specific pharmacology.

Module 2.4 must address competing mechanistic hypotheses and their implications for human risk extrapolation. If the tumor response reflects pharmacologic hazard (adverse interpretation), the absence of a NOAEL in semaglutide and dulaglutide rat studies and tumor occurrence at sub-clinical exposures (semaglutide at 0.8-fold MRHD with carcinomas at ≥0.8-fold) [R-5, R-6] require discussion of margin-of-exposure adequacy and whether linear or threshold dose-response models apply. The progression from adenoma to carcinoma in rats and mice [R-1, R-5, R-6] and the treatment-duration dependency [R-3] suggest cumulative genotoxic or mitogenic mechanisms, yet all compounds tested negative in standard genotoxicity batteries [R-5, R-6, R-8], presenting an apparent contradiction that Module 2.4 must reconcile. If species-specific receptor expression differences account for the rodent susceptibility (non-adverse/artifact interpretations), Module 2.4 must present comparative GLP-1R expression data for rodent versus human thyroid C-cells—data not evident in the provided excerpts—and explain why regulatory agencies required boxed warnings despite this proposed mechanistic disconnect [R-1, R-3, R-5, R-6]. The exenatide study showing female-specific adenomas at all doses including 5-fold human exposure [R-8] versus tirzepatide showing tumors in both sexes at 0.1-fold human exposure [R-2] present discordant sex and dose patterns requiring integrated class-level interpretation rather than compound-by-compound assessment.

The GLP-1R knockout phenotype—viable, fertile, mild glucose intolerance, no thyroid pathology [R-9]—addresses receptor loss-of-function and does not inform the gain-of-function tumor mechanism operative under chronic agonism; Module 2.4 must maintain this conceptual separation to avoid inappropriate conflation of loss-of-function safety with chronic pharmacologic overstimulation risk. If adaptive interpretation applies (compensatory cascade with threshold), Module 2.4 must define proposed thresholds and reconcile them with semaglutide data showing adenomas at the lowest tested dose [R-5, R-6] and must specify intermediate-timepoint biomarkers (C-cell hyperplasia severity grading, proliferation indices, calcitonin secretion kinetics) that would validate threshold versus linear kinetics. No evidence of extended-duration nonclinical studies beyond 2 years or of GLP-1R expression mapping in human thyroid tissue was found in the excerpts; Module 2.4 must state whether such studies exist in sponsor files or represent data gaps.

The liraglutide fibrosarcoma finding at injection sites in male mice [R-1] and insulin glargine histiocytomas at injection sites in male rats [R-7], both attributed to local formulation effects and vehicle composition respectively, require Module 2.4 discussion of whether local tissue irritation from high-concentration GLP-1R agonist formulations (liraglutide clinical formulation 10-fold higher than carcinogenicity study formulation [R-1]) constitutes an independent risk factor distinct from systemic C-cell effects. The fertility findings in tirzepatide females (prolonged diestrus, decreased corpora lutea, decreased implantation sites and viable embryos at all doses [R-2]) attributed to pharmacology require Module 2.4 integration with the reproductive toxicology section to clarify whether these effects represent direct ovarian GLP-1R engagement or secondary metabolic consequences, given the knockout phenotype shows normal fertility [R-9]. Module 2.4 must explicitly state that NOAEL determination and acceptability judgments for the C-cell tumor finding remain the responsibility of the regulatory toxicologist and clinical-nonclinical integration team, not resolved by the nonclinical data summary alone.
