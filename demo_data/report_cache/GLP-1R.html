1. Target Profile & Biological Function

GLP-1R (glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor) is a G protein-coupled receptor that mediates the incretin effects of the endogenous hormone GLP-1. Evidence from Glp1r-null mice demonstrates that the receptor plays a physiological role in glucose homeostasis and insulin secretion; knockout animals are viable and fertile with normal body weight on standard chow but exhibit mild glucose intolerance and a blunted insulin secretory response to oral glucose, consistent with loss of incretin signaling [R-9]. The receptor also influences gastric emptying kinetics [R-9]. The viable, mild phenotype of null animals indicates that loss of GLP-1R function is not developmentally catastrophic or embryonically lethal [R-9].

GLP-1R is the molecular target for an approved drug class comprising glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists used primarily for the treatment of type 2 diabetes mellitus and obesity. Approved drugs acting as GLP-1R agonists include liraglutide [R-1], semaglutide [R-6], dulaglutide [R-3], exenatide [R-8], and lixisenatide [R-7], as well as the dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist tirzepatide [R-2]. These agents are administered by subcutaneous injection or, in the case of oral semaglutide, via oral tablet formulation [R-5]. Fixed-dose combination products pairing GLP-1R agonists with basal insulins have also been approved, including insulin degludec/liraglutide [R-4] and insulin glargine/lixisenatide [R-7].

The pharmacologic mechanism of GLP-1R agonism—chronic receptor activation—produces effects distinct from receptor loss-of-function, and the knockout precedent does not address consequences of sustained agonism [R-9].

2. Genetic & Knockout Precedent

**Section 2: Genetic & Knockout Precedent**

A curated Glp1r-null mouse model (GEMOCKT/KURATIERT) demonstrates that complete loss of GLP-1R function is compatible with viability, fertility, and normal body weight on standard chow [R-9]. Knockout animals exhibit mild glucose intolerance and blunted insulin secretion in response to oral glucose, consistent with GLP-1R's incretin role in glucoregulation, as well as altered gastric emptying kinetics; no embryonic lethality or major structural malformations are reported [R-9]. The viable, mild knockout phenotype indicates that loss of GLP-1R function itself is not developmentally catastrophic and supports interpreting on-target metabolic effects such as gastrointestinal and glycemic changes as manageable or adaptive phenomena rather than fundamental developmental liabilities [R-9].

The rodent thyroid C-cell tumors observed across multiple GLP-1R agonist labels—including liraglutide [R-1], tirzepatide [R-2], dulaglutide [R-3], semaglutide [R-5, R-6], and exenatide [R-8]—arise from chronic pharmacologic receptor agonism at systemic exposures, not from loss of receptor function [R-9]. This thyroid finding is therefore not illuminated by knockout precedent, which models loss-of-function, not gain-of-function [R-9]. The distinction is critical for regulatory interpretation: the curated knockout data address whether absence of GLP-1R is tolerable, whereas the carcinogenicity findings reflect sustained GLP-1R activation. The viable knockout model does not predict or contradict the rodent-specific neoplasia observed with chronic agonist exposure and should not be conflated with the pharmacologic-exposure safety profile in this context [R-9].

3. Class Effects & Known Target Toxicities

Thyroid C-cell tumors constitute the predominant class-effect toxicity across GLP-1R agonists. Statistically significant increases in thyroid C-cell adenomas were observed in rodent carcinogenicity studies for liraglutide [R-1], tirzepatide [R-2], dulaglutide [R-3], semaglutide [R-5, R-6], and exenatide [R-8]. C-cell carcinomas were documented for liraglutide (3% of females at 3 mg/kg/day) [R-1], tirzepatide (adenomas and carcinomas combined at all doses examined in male and female rats) [R-2], dulaglutide (numerical increases at 24-fold human exposure) [R-3], and semaglutide (statistically significant increase in males at ≥0.01 mg/kg/day at clinically relevant exposures) [R-5, R-6]. For multiple agents, these tumors appeared at exposures equivalent to or below the maximum recommended human dose, with tirzepatide showing adenomas in females at 0.1-fold human exposure [R-2] and semaglutide showing carcinomas in males at 0.8-fold human exposure [R-5, R-6]. The finding is characterized as dose-dependent and treatment-duration-dependent, occurring in both genders across multiple rodent species (rats and mice) [R-1].

Injection-site fibrosarcomas represent a second recurring pattern. Liraglutide caused treatment-related fibrosarcomas on the dorsal skin and subcutis in males at 3 mg/kg/day, attributed to high local drug concentration near the injection site [R-1]. The clinical formulation concentration (6 mg/mL) exceeded the concentration used in the mouse carcinogenicity study (0.6 mg/mL) by 10-fold [R-1]. Insulin glargine (co-administered with lixisenatide, a GLP-1R agonist) produced statistically significant histiocytomas at injection sites in male rats [R-7].

Reproductive effects occurred in tirzepatide studies, with female rats exhibiting prolonged diestrus, decreased corpora lutea, and reduced implantation sites and viable embryos at all dose levels, effects considered secondary to the pharmacological activity [R-2]. Human relevance of rodent thyroid C-cell tumors remains undetermined and could not be established by clinical or nonclinical studies [R-3, R-5, R-6].

4. Regulatory & Clinical Precedents

The GLP-1 receptor (GLP-1R) has supported approval of multiple drugs across both diabetes and obesity indications, with a consistent regulatory focus on a shared nonclinical finding. All GLP-1R agonists that have undergone standard rodent carcinogenicity testing—including liraglutide, semaglutide, dulaglutide, tirzepatide, exenatide, and lixisenatide—demonstrated statistically significant, dose-dependent increases in thyroid C-cell tumors (adenomas and/or carcinomas) in rats and/or mice at exposures ranging from clinically relevant to multiples of the maximum recommended human dose [R-1, R-2, R-3, R-5, R-6, R-8]. Liraglutide, semaglutide (both oral and injectable), dulaglutide, and tirzepatide each carry a Boxed Warning for risk of thyroid C-cell tumors, with identical language stating that the human relevance of liraglutide-induced (or respective drug-induced) rodent thyroid C-cell tumors has not been determined, and that it is unknown whether these agents cause medullary thyroid carcinoma in humans [R-1, R-2, R-3, R-5, R-6]. All labeled products within this class contraindicate use in patients with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia syndrome type 2 (MEN 2), and require patient counseling on symptoms of thyroid tumors such as neck mass, dysphagia, dyspnea, or persistent hoarseness [R-1, R-2, R-3, R-5, R-6]. The labeling uniformly notes that routine serum calcitonin monitoring or thyroid ultrasound is of uncertain value for early detection of medullary thyroid carcinoma in patients treated with these agents [R-1, R-5, R-6]. No evidence of thyroid C-cell hyperplasia or neoplasia was seen in a 6-month rasH2 transgenic mouse study of dulaglutide [R-3] or tirzepatide [R-2], though the standard 2-year rat studies for both molecules showed positive findings.

Specific application numbers and detailed approval timelines are not provided in the excerpts. The combination products Xultophy 100/3.6 (insulin degludec/liraglutide) and Soliqua 100/33 (insulin glargine/lixisenatide) reference the nonclinical data of their individual components rather than conducting separate carcinogenicity programs for the fixed-dose combinations [R-4, R-7]. Additional major safety concerns beyond the thyroid C-cell tumor boxed warning are not detailed in the nonclinical toxicology excerpts provided. The class has achieved regulatory success across multiple agents and indications despite the consistent rodent oncogenicity signal, reflecting a regulatory determination that the uncertain human relevance of the rodent finding and the availability of risk mitigation through contraindications and patient counseling support approval with prominent labeling.

5. Adversity & Evidence Weight Analysis

Four independent interpretations of the primary finding are presented below, each citing only the evidence provided. No single interpretation is presented as this tool's recommendation -- that judgment belongs to the reviewing toxicologist.

adverse — strongest grounding

GLP-1R agonists consistently produce dose-dependent and treatment-duration-dependent thyroid C-cell adenomas and carcinomas across multiple compounds (liraglutide, tirzepatide, dulaglutide, semaglutide, exenatide) in both rats and mice at clinically relevant exposures, with statistically significant increases observed even at the lowest doses tested [R-1, R-2, R-3, R-5, R-6, R-8]. Liraglutide induced malignant C-cell carcinomas in 3% of female mice at 45-times human exposure and benign adenomas at incidences up to 20%, with similar tumor patterns replicated across the entire drug class at exposures ranging from sub-clinical to 113-fold the maximum recommended human dose [R-1, R-5, R-6]. The consistent reproducibility of these neoplastic findings across species, compounds, and dose ranges, combined with their occurrence at clinically relevant exposures, establishes thyroid C-cell tumorigenesis as a clear on-target pharmacologic hazard requiring contraindication in at-risk patients and ongoing vigilance for medullary thyroid carcinoma in the general treatment population [R-1, R-2, R-3, R-5, R-6, R-8].

non-adverse

The thyroid C-cell tumors observed consistently across GLP-1R agonists in rodents do not represent a clinically meaningful adverse effect because the human relevance of these rodent findings is unknown and could not be determined by clinical or nonclinical studies [R-3, R-5, R-6]. Rodent C-cells express high levels of GLP-1R and are uniquely susceptible to proliferative stimulation, whereas human C-cells have minimal to absent GLP-1R expression, representing a fundamental species difference in target tissue distribution. The findings occur at exposures as low as clinically relevant levels in rats [R-5, R-6] yet no signal of medullary thyroid carcinoma has emerged in extensive human clinical experience across multiple approved GLP-1R agonists [R-1, R-2, R-3, R-8], supporting that this is a rodent-specific, non-translatable pharmacologic response rather than a true toxicologic hazard. The viable, fertile Glp1r-null mouse phenotype with only mild metabolic effects [R-9] further demonstrates that GLP-1R pathway perturbation does not produce catastrophic biological consequences, distinguishing routine pharmacologic receptor engagement from genuine toxicity.

6. Nonclinical Safety Recommendations & CTD-M2.4-Bridge

Thyroid C-cell adenomas and carcinomas occurred in chronic rodent carcinogenicity studies across multiple GLP-1R agonists—liraglutide, semaglutide, dulaglutide, tirzepatide, and exenatide—at exposures ranging from clinically relevant (≥0.8× human AUC) to supra-therapeutic [R-1, R-2, R-3, R-5, R-6, R-8]. Incidences were statistically significant in both sexes, dose-dependent, and treatment-duration-dependent, with progression to carcinoma documented in multiple studies [R-1, R-2, R-3, R-5, R-6]. All labels carry boxed warnings stating human relevance is unknown and could not be determined by clinical or nonclinical studies; contraindications apply to patients with personal/family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia syndrome type 2 [R-1, R-3, R-5, R-6]. Glp1r-null mice are viable, fertile, and exhibit only mild glucose intolerance, indicating loss-of-function is non-catastrophic and supporting on-target metabolic effects as manageable; however, the knockout does not address the gain-of-function (agonist-driven) thyroid C-cell proliferation observed with chronic receptor stimulation [R-9].

**If interpreted as adverse carcinogenicity**: CTD Module 2.4 must comprehensively document tumor incidence tables, statistical analyses, dose-response relationships, latency, progression rates (adenoma vs. carcinoma), and tissue GLP-1R expression data (rodent vs. human C-cells) to frame the weight-of-evidence for or against human translatability. Comparative cross-species receptor-distribution studies and mechanistic investigations (e.g., calcitonin secretion, C-cell proliferation markers in short-term studies, receptor occupancy modeling) would address whether the rodent thyroid is a susceptible outlier. Clinical monitoring strategies (serum calcitonin, thyroid ultrasound) and their uncertain predictive value must be discussed [R-1], alongside post-marketing epidemiologic surveillance plans to detect any human MTC signal. The consistency across five structurally distinct molecules [R-1, R-2, R-3, R-5, R-6, R-8] and occurrence of frank carcinomas [R-1, R-2, R-5, R-6] necessitate detailed justification if a NOAEL for human risk assessment is proposed.

**If interpreted as non-adverse exaggerated pharmacology**: Module 2.4 must explicitly justify why tumor findings are discounted for human risk, citing quantitative GLP-1R expression differences between rodent and human C-cells, lack of genotoxicity [R-5, R-6, R-8], mechanistic data on species-specific calcitonin-proliferation coupling, and absence of human MTC signal in post-marketing databases. The argument must address why regulatory precedent nonetheless required boxed warnings if the findings are biologically irrelevant. Injection-site fibrosarcomas attributed to high local drug concentration in liraglutide mice [R-1] exemplify formulation-dependent artifacts distinct from systemic carcinogenicity; Module 2.4 should clarify whether C-cell tumors similarly reflect experimental design choices (bolus dosing, exposure duration exceeding therapeutic use) or intrinsic pharmacology.

**If interpreted as adaptive hyperplasia progressing to neoplasia**: Module 2.4 must distinguish compensatory C-cell proliferation (a homeostatic response to chronic GLP-1R stimulation) from primary genotoxic carcinogenesis, supported by negative mutagenicity/clastogenicity batteries [R-5, R-6, R-8]. The 2-year rodent study duration far exceeds typical human treatment windows; time-to-tumor data and reversibility studies (if conducted) would inform whether tumors represent an endpoint of prolonged adaptive overshoot rather than irreversible transformation. Patient selection criteria (MEN2 exclusion) appropriately mitigate risk in populations with baseline C-cell hyperplasia, but Module 2.4 should address whether similar adaptive mechanisms could theoretically operate in human thyroid under chronic agonism, even if GLP-1R expression is lower.

**If interpreted as artifact**: Module 2.4 must critically evaluate study design variables—formulation concentration [R-1], injection-site confounds [R-1, R-7], strain-specific baseline tumor rates, and statistical anomalies (significance at all doses including sub-therapeutic [R-5, R-6])—and present bridging data distinguishing true liability from experimental noise. The absence of C-cell findings in dulaglutide rasH2 transgenic mice [R-3] and exenatide CD-1 mice [R-8], despite positive rat findings, suggests species/strain variability that may reflect technical rather than pharmacologic factors. However, Module 2.4 must reconcile why artifact hypotheses did not prevent class-wide boxed warnings, and whether vehicle controls, formulation-matched comparators, or alternative dosing regimens were explored.

No NOAEL for thyroid C-cell effects can be established, as tumors occurred at the lowest doses tested in rat studies for semaglutide (below quantifiable exposure to 0.8× human AUC) and at all doses for other molecules [R-2, R-3, R-5, R-6, R-8]. Fertility was unaffected in male rats across the class; decreased corpora lutea and implantation sites in tirzepatide-treated female rats were considered secondary to pharmacology-driven body-weight/metabolic changes, not direct reproductive toxicity [R-2, R-3, R-5, R-6, R-8]. The critical outstanding question for Module 2.4 is whether additional mechanistic studies—human C-cell primary culture agonist exposure, GLP-1R occupancy-tumor modeling, extended juvenile studies if pediatric development is planned—would substantively alter the risk-benefit calculus, or whether the existing evidence envelope (rodent signal plus human surveillance showing no validated MTC cases to date) is considered sufficient given class regulatory precedent and the "uncertain value" of prospective clinical monitoring [R-1].
